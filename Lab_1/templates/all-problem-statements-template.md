## Nino Tsutskiridze

### H1 – Hairband (a problem in your own daily life)

**Problem:**  
KIU students who want private tutoring in a specific subject spend 2–3 hours per search attempt trying to find a suitable tutor, because recommendations happen entirely through personal connections and word-of-mouth group chats, and a tutor who is available and affordable for one student is often already full or charges a different rate by the time another student asks.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
I needed a maths tutor at the start of this semester. I asked in three different group chats, got five names, and by the time I contacted them, two were full, one had raised their rate, and one only taught at times I was in lecture. The whole process took me the better part of a week.

**Rough severity estimate:** High  
The wasted time is significant, but the bigger problem is that students who fail to find a tutor quickly enough simply go without, which compounds into worse exam results.

---

### H2 – Headache (a problem you observed in someone else)

**Problem:**  
Secondary school students in Tbilisi who attend private tutoring sessions lose an average of 10–15 minutes at the start of each session because their tutor uses the opening minutes to re-establish where they left off, having no written record of what was covered or what the student struggled with in the previous lesson.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
My younger sister attends private English tutoring twice a week. I have dropped her off and picked her up on multiple occasions and she consistently reports that the first part of each lesson is the tutor asking "so what did we do last time?" and her trying to reconstruct it from memory. She said her tutor admitted she keeps no notes between students.

**Rough severity estimate:** Medium  
Over a semester of twice-weekly lessons, this adds up to several full sessions' worth of lost instruction time. It also means recurring mistakes are not systematically tracked.

---

### H3 – Heartburn (a recurring problem in a community or context you know)

**Problem:**  
University students at KIU who tutor classmates or younger students informally for cash consistently undercharge for their time because they have no reference point for what a fair rate is, and they feel uncomfortable raising their price with an existing student even after realising their rate is below market, leading them to either absorb the loss or quietly stop taking new students in that subject.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
I know at least five people at KIU who tutor privately. Every conversation we have had about it includes some version of "I don't know if I'm charging too little." Two of them have told me directly they have not raised their rate in over a year despite becoming significantly more experienced, because they feel awkward bringing it up with families they already have a relationship with.

**Rough severity estimate:** High  
Tutors who are being paid below what their time is worth either burn out or quietly exit the market. Students lose access to good, affordable tutors. The pricing opacity hurts both sides.

## Lizi Margvelashvili

### H1 – Hairband

**Problem:**  
University students who learn a subject through a private tutor lose continuity between sessions when they review their notes afterward, because their notes are a mix of their own scribbles and verbal explanations the tutor gave that are not written anywhere, and by the next session they cannot reconstruct the reasoning behind a method they were shown.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
I study statistics with a private tutor once a week. After every session I feel like I understood everything, but when I sit down two days later to do practice problems I realise I only remember the steps and not the reasoning. I have had to ask the same "why does this work?" question in three consecutive sessions because I had not captured the explanation the first two times.

**Rough severity estimate:** Medium  
The immediate consequence is re-covering material unnecessarily. The longer-term consequence is shallower understanding because reasoning is never retained, only procedure.

---

### H2 – Headache

**Problem:**  
Parents of secondary school students in Tbilisi who hire a private tutor for their child have no reliable way to know whether the sessions are actually effective, because the tutor reports progress verbally and informally at pickup, and the only external signal is the child's next test score — which arrives weeks after the tutoring has already continued.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
My neighbour hires a tutor for her 14-year-old daughter for Georgian language. She told me she has paid for three months of weekly sessions and only found out the tutor had been reteaching the same grammar unit for six weeks when her daughter's grade on a class test did not improve. She had no way to know this was happening because her daughter did not tell her and the tutor only ever said "she's doing well, we're making progress."

**Rough severity estimate:** High  
Parents are paying for a service they cannot evaluate. The consequence is wasted money, a student who is not progressing, and a trust breakdown when the problem surfaces too late to course-correct.

---

### H3 – Heartburn

**Problem:**  
Private tutors in Georgia who work with multiple students across different subjects and age groups spend 30–45 minutes per week on scheduling logistics — rescheduling via WhatsApp, confirming times, handling last-minute cancellations — because they have no shared tool with their students and every change is a separate negotiation over text.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
I have two cousins who both tutor privately. Both of them have described their WhatsApp as "a second job" specifically because of scheduling. One of them showed me her phone during a family dinner — she had 14 unread messages that were all about rescheduling or confirming upcoming sessions across six different student family chats.

**Rough severity estimate:** High  
The scheduling overhead is a direct tax on time that should be spent preparing lessons or resting. Both cousins have said they have turned down new students because managing more schedules feels unsustainable.

## Luka Khimshiashvili

### H1 – Hairband

**Problem:**  
CS students at KIU who fall behind in a core programming course during the first four weeks of semester struggle to find a peer tutor who understands both the subject and their specific knowledge gap, because the only available channels are group chats where asking for help publicly feels exposing, and private requests rely on knowing who is strong enough to help.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
In my first semester I fell behind in Fundamentals of Programming in week three. I did not want to post in the main group chat because I did not want to look like I was struggling. I ended up approaching someone I knew personally from orientation — but if I had not known him I would not have known who to ask. I later found out two other students in my cohort had the same problem and also did not reach out.

**Rough severity estimate:** High  
Falling behind in a core course in the first month has compounding consequences. Students who cannot find help quietly fall further behind rather than risking the social exposure of asking publicly.

---

### H2 – Headache

**Problem:**  
First-year students who are assigned a peer mentor through KIU's formal mentoring programme lose the benefit of the relationship within the first 4–5 weeks because neither the mentor nor the mentee has a structured agenda for sessions, and once the initial orientation questions are answered, neither party knows what to do with the time.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
I was assigned a mentor in my first semester and I attended three sessions before we both stopped scheduling them. It was not because the mentor was unhelpful — it was because neither of us knew what to talk about after the practical questions were answered. I spoke to my mentor about it later and he said the same pattern happened with two other mentees he had that year.

**Rough severity estimate:** Medium  
Mentoring relationships that collapse early leave first-year students without a structured support system at exactly the point in the semester when coursework starts to increase in difficulty.

---

### H3 – Heartburn

**Problem:**  
Private tutors who teach technical subjects (mathematics, programming, physics) to secondary school students in Georgia find it difficult to accurately assess a new student's level in the first session, because they have no standardised way to do a diagnostic, and the student's self-reported level ("I'm okay at algebra") is consistently misaligned with their actual ability, leading to the first two or three sessions being wasted on content that is either too easy or too advanced.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
Two people I know who tutor maths privately have both described this problem independently. One of them told me she now spends the entire first session just doing informal diagnostic problems before she makes any plan, which means the family pays for a session that produces no actual teaching. She said she has had students who told her they were "fine with quadratics" who could not solve a linear equation.

**Rough severity estimate:** High  
Wasted early sessions are a direct financial loss for the family and create a bad first impression that affects the tutor-student relationship. Tutors who do not catch the misalignment early end up teaching at the wrong level for weeks.

## Mari Janjghava

### H1 – Hairband

**Problem:**  
University students who need to improve their English for academic writing spend 3–4 weeks searching for an affordable tutor who specialises in academic writing rather than general conversational English, because most tutors available through informal channels teach general English and the distinction is not visible until the first session.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
I needed help with academic writing in English this semester specifically — not grammar, not conversation, but how to structure arguments and use academic register. I found three tutors through classmates. The first two taught general English and were not useful for my specific need. The third was what I was looking for, but I only found her because she happened to mention her background in a mutual friend's group chat.

**Rough severity estimate:** Medium  
The search cost is real, but the larger problem is that students who cannot find a specialist tutor default to general English tutors who cannot actually help with their need, and they only discover this after paying for one or two sessions.

---

### H2 – Headache

**Problem:**  
Secondary school students in Kutaisi preparing for university entrance examinations lose 20–30 minutes per tutoring session to content that is not relevant to their specific exam, because their tutor uses a general subject curriculum rather than an exam-specific one, and the student does not have enough knowledge to redirect the session toward what is actually tested.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
My younger brother is preparing for the national university entrance exam in mathematics. His tutor is genuinely strong in maths but is teaching from a general secondary school curriculum. My brother mentioned that his tutor spent two sessions on topics that do not appear on the national exam. When I looked at the exam format myself and pointed this out, the tutor acknowledged he was not familiar with the specific exam structure.

**Rough severity estimate:** High  
Students preparing for entrance examinations have a fixed and finite time window. Sessions that cover irrelevant content directly reduce the preparation time available for what is actually tested.

---

### H3 – Heartburn

**Problem:**  
Private language tutors in Georgia who teach multiple students per week lose track of individual student progress across sessions because their only record-keeping system is a personal notebook that is not shared with students or parents, and when a student misses a session or the tutor is unavailable, the continuity of the learning plan is broken with no easy way to recover it.

**Where this observation comes from:**  
I tutored English privately for eight months while at school. I used a handwritten notebook for each student. When one student missed three sessions due to illness and came back, I could not remember exactly where we had stopped or what exercises she had completed. I improvised for that session and later realised I had retaught something she already knew. I also had no way to share progress with her parents, who occasionally asked how she was doing, because my notes were not formatted for that purpose.

**Rough severity estimate:** High  
The consequence for tutors is lost preparation time and repeated content. The consequence for students and parents is a learning experience that feels disorganised and unaccountable, which erodes trust in the tutor.
