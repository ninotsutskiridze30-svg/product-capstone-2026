## Detailed Pattern Analysis

### Pattern 1: Total information invisibility — every data point requires a separate manual conversation (Rank #1)

**Frequency:** Mentioned in 8 of 8 interviews (Interviews: TK, AB, GM, SN, MT, DK, NA, LG)

**Pain Intensity:** 5 / 5

**Supporting quotes:**

1. "Everything. I did not know if they were any good, I did not know their rate, I did not know when they were free. You find out all of that by messaging them." (Interview 1, TK)
2. "Rates change, availability changes, nothing is written down anywhere. Every time you start a search it is like starting from scratch because nothing you knew before is still true." (Interview 3, GM)
3. "I can see people in my year who tutor. I just don't know who they are or how to reach them." (Interview 8, LG)
4. "I get messages from people who want subjects I do not teach, or who cannot afford my rate, or who need a time I do not have. It wastes both our time." (Interview 5, MT — same pattern from the tutor's side)

**Emotional language observed:** Resignation ("there is no other way"), frustration ("it wastes both our time"), normalisation ("that's just how it works here"). No interviewee expressed surprise that the information was unavailable — they have accepted its absence as a fixed condition.

**Consequences reported:**
- Time impact: 1–6 weeks of active search time for a single tutor. GM contacted four people before a match. SN spent six weeks before abandoning.
- Financial impact: DK committed to a first session before being able to assess quality — paid for an unknown. MT and tutors like her invest time in conversations with students who turn out to be unmatched.
- Emotional impact: DK described the quality-unknown as "the most anxiety-producing part." AB described being mid-search with no remaining options and "not knowing what to do next."
- Academic impact: The information search competes directly with the academic work the student is already behind on. GM: "You fall further behind while you are trying to catch up."

#### Five Whys for Pattern 1

**Surface problem:** Students cannot find out a tutor's availability, rate, or subject coverage without messaging them individually.

**Why #1:** Why can't students find this information without messaging?
Because there is no shared record or profile where tutors publish this information.

**Why #2:** Why is there no shared record of tutor information?
Because tutors have never needed to maintain one — all discovery has always happened through personal referral, which requires no public profile.

**Why #3:** Why has all discovery always happened through personal referral?
Because there is no Georgian tutoring marketplace, and the social channels (WhatsApp, Messenger) that students use for other coordination purposes became the default discovery channel by proximity, not design.

**Why #4:** Why has no Georgian tutoring marketplace emerged?
Because the market is informal, geographically distributed, and dominated by individual arrangements that do not generate the visible transaction data that would attract a platform operator.

**Why #5:** Why does the informality of the market persist?
Because neither students nor tutors have had a tool that made formalisation easy — tutors would need to invest time maintaining a profile, and students have adapted (however poorly) to the current system.

**Root cause identified:** The Georgian private tutoring market has no coordination infrastructure — no shared layer where tutor status is visible, queryable, or maintained. The market functions entirely through personal social graphs, which are opaque, non-transferable, and inaccessible to anyone outside an established network.

#### Current Workarounds

| Workaround | Who Uses It | How Well It Works | Why It Falls Short |
|-----------|------------|------------------|-------------------|
| Group chat post requesting a tutor name | TK, AB, GM, SN, NA, LG (all student interviews) | Low — inconsistent responses, stale names, no information attached | Names arrive without rate, availability, or quality context; response rate varies; pool depletes after 1–2 posts |
| Direct ask to one trusted person for a personal introduction | TK, GM, DK | Medium-high — personal vouching drives conversion | Available only to students with a relevant social connection; does not scale; the connection may not know anyone currently available |
| Facebook group / online platform search | SN, NA | Very low — listings stale or language-inaccessible | Georgian tutors are not listed; platforms designed for other markets; Georgian-language platforms exclude non-Georgian speakers |
| Asking a professor or faculty member | AB (considered but avoided) | Potentially medium | Requires disclosing academic difficulty to a person in authority — social cost is high enough that most students avoid this channel |

### Pattern 2: Serial restart dynamic — each failed contact resets the search to zero (Rank #2)

**Frequency:** Mentioned in 7 of 8 interviews (Interviews: TK, AB, GM, SN, DK, NA, LG; not applicable to MT as a tutor-side interview)

**Pain Intensity:** 4 / 5

**Supporting quotes:**

1. "Two were full, one had changed their rate to something I could not afford, one just did not reply. The fourth one worked out." (Interview 3, GM)
2. "I did not make a decision to stop. I just stopped. There was nobody left to ask." (Interview 4, SN)
3. "I posted twice in the CS group. The second time I wrote I was getting desperate." (Interview 8, LG)
4. "I messaged her, she does general English, not academic writing. That is not what I need... I do not know what to do next." (Interview 2, AB)

**Emotional language observed:** Exhaustion ("I just stopped"), desperation (LG's second post), resignation (GM describing four contacts as normal for an experienced searcher), helplessness ("I do not know what to do next").

**Consequences reported:**
- Time impact: Each restart adds days to the search. TK's search took 1.5 weeks; SN's took 6 weeks before abandonment.
- Academic impact: SN failed a first exam attempt directly attributable to not finding a tutor before the assessment.
- Emotional impact: The cumulative effect of repeated restarts is progressive demoralisation. AB's mid-search state ("I do not know what to do next") captures the endpoint of this process before abandonment.
- Abandonment: TK's roommate stopped searching and sat an exam without support. SN abandoned after 6 weeks.

#### Five Whys for Pattern 2

**Surface problem:** When a tutor contact fails, students have to start the search over.

**Why #1:** Why does a failed contact require starting over?
Because the group chat is a flat list of names — when a name fails, there is no next filtered option, only a return to the same pool.

**Why #2:** Why is there no next filtered option?
Because the search channel (group chat) has no filtering, sorting, or memory — it cannot carry forward the information that this student needs X subject at Y rate at Z time, and show only tutors who match.

**Why #3:** Why does the group chat have no filtering or memory?
Because group chats are general-purpose communication tools, not search tools. They were never designed for this use case.

**Why #4:** Why are students using general-purpose communication tools for structured search?
Because no purpose-built search tool exists for the Georgian tutoring market.

**Why #5:** Why does no purpose-built tool exist?
Same root cause as Pattern 1 — the market is informal, the transaction volume is invisible, and no operator has built the coordination layer.

**Root cause identified:** The same absence of market infrastructure that produces Pattern 1 also produces Pattern 2. Without a persistent, filterable record of tutor status, every search must proceed from scratch regardless of prior attempts. The restart is not a failure of student effort — it is the inevitable consequence of using a communication tool as a search tool.

#### Current Workarounds

| Workaround | Who Uses It | How Well It Works | Why It Falls Short |
|-----------|------------|------------------|-------------------|
| Posting in the group chat a second time | LG, AB | Low | Same depleted pool; diminishing returns on repeated posts |
| Broadening the network (asking outside KIU, asking family contacts) | DK (family in Tbilisi), NA (TSU contacts) | Medium — produces new names from outside the primary pool | Accidental rather than systematic; not reproducible for students without cross-institutional connections |
| Accepting a suboptimal match to avoid restarting | TK, GM | Stops the restart but lowers match quality | Wrong schedule, higher rate, or wrong subject specialisation accepted as "good enough" |

### Pattern 3: Trust gap — a name without personal vouching has low or no conversion value (Rank #3)

**Frequency:** Mentioned in 5 of 8 interviews (Interviews: TK, AB, GM, DK, SN)

**Pain Intensity:** 4 / 5

**Supporting quotes:**

1. "If my friend had not given me the last name directly and said she had used her personally, I would have stopped. I just needed someone to vouch for them." (Interview 1, TK)
2. "Someone gave me a name but they were not sure if that person still tutored." (Interview 4, SN — a name without confidence is nearly useless)
3. "The worst part is you don't know if the person is any good until you've already committed to a session and paid for it." (Interview 6, DK)
4. AB described wanting "reviews maybe" unprompted when asked to describe the ideal search outcome (Interview 2)

**Emotional language observed:** Anxiety before the first session (DK), relief when a trusted voucher was available (TK), distrust of unverified names (SN).

**Consequences reported:**
- Conversion failure: Students who receive names without endorsements often do not contact them, or contact them with low confidence. The name-without-voucher has low signal value.
- Financial risk: DK paid for a session before being able to assess quality. The first session is both a financial and time commitment made with incomplete information.
- Search prolongation: Students who receive names they do not trust continue searching rather than acting, extending the search even when nominal options exist.

#### Five Whys for Pattern 3

**Surface problem:** Students will not act on a tutor recommendation unless someone they trust has personally used that tutor.

**Why #1:** Why is personal vouching required for conversion?
Because there is no other quality signal available — no reviews, no ratings, no verified credentials, no past student feedback.

**Why #2:** Why are there no quality signals for tutors?
Because tutors operate informally with no public presence and no record of past engagements that could generate a quality signal.

**Why #3:** Why do tutors have no public presence or record?
Because the market has always operated through personal referral, which requires no public profile and generates no persistent quality data.

**Why #4:** Why has the market always operated through personal referral?
Because no platform has existed to aggregate tutor profiles and the feedback from student sessions.

**Why #5:** Why has no such platform existed?
Root cause — same as Patterns 1 and 2: the Georgian tutoring market has no coordination infrastructure, and without a platform to collect session outcomes, quality signals cannot accumulate.

**Root cause identified:** Quality information about tutors is not generated, stored, or accessible in the current market. Every student starts from zero knowledge of a tutor's quality, regardless of how many students that tutor has worked with before. A solution that only solves discoverability without building a trust layer will face the same conversion problem that personal referral currently solves inefficiently.

#### Current Workarounds

| Workaround | Who Uses It | Why It Falls Short |
|-----------|------------|-------------------|
| Waiting for a personal vouching referral from a trusted contact | TK, GM, DK | Only available to students with the right social connection; cannot be manufactured; not available to international students or new arrivals |
| Asking the tutor's rate after first contact as a proxy for quality ("cheaper = less experienced") | GM (inferred from rate discussion) | Unreliable proxy; some inexperienced tutors charge high rates; some excellent tutors charge low rates |
| Paying for a first session to assess quality directly | DK | Financial and time cost for what is essentially a quality test; still no guarantee |

### Pattern 4: Bilateral coordination failure — tutors are equally information-poor and receive redundant mismatched demand (Rank #4)

**Frequency:** Mentioned in 3 of 8 interviews (Interviews: MT directly; GM and LG via proxy references to tutors they know)

**Pain Intensity:** 3 / 5

**Key supporting quotes:**

1. "I am guessing just like they are." (Interview 5, MT — describing trying to refer a declined student to another tutor)
2. "I would like to say somewhere: I have two spots left, I teach second-year maths, I charge 35 GEL per hour. And then people who fit that can message me." (Interview 5, MT — direct product specification)
3. "My tutor told me she had three people message her the same week I did. She had to say no to two of them." (Interview 3, GM — tutor-side burden described by a student)

**Root cause (abbreviated):** The same absence of a shared coordination layer that makes students information-poor also makes tutors information-poor. Tutors cannot broadcast their status, so they receive unfiltered inbound demand they must process individually. This is the supply-side mirror of the student-side problem.

**Consequence for product direction:** A solution that only serves students (making tutors findable) without serving tutors (reducing inbox noise and enabling filtering) will face a supply adoption problem. Tutors are the supply side of a two-sided market. If using the platform does not reduce their coordination burden, they have no incentive to maintain a profile.

### Pattern 5: Network access inequality — students without an established Georgian social network have no starting point (Rank #5)

**Frequency:** Mentioned in 3 of 8 interviews (Interviews: SN directly; AB via Azerbaijani friends; NA via cross-university discovery)

**Pain Intensity:** 5 / 5

**Key supporting quotes:**

1. "I did not make a decision to stop. I just stopped. There was nobody left to ask." (Interview 4, SN)
2. "She gave me a name but told me the tutor only speaks Georgian. I do not speak Georgian well enough for a maths lesson." (Interview 4, SN)
3. AB mentioned two friends from Azerbaijan who experienced the same problem last semester — one never found a tutor (Interview 2, corroboration)

**Root cause (abbreviated):** The word-of-mouth discovery system is built entirely on existing social graphs. A student who arrived in Georgia 6–18 months ago, primarily knows other international students, and does not speak Georgian fluently has a social graph that produces almost no tutor names — and the names it does produce are often language-inaccessible. The current system does not just work poorly for these students; it functionally does not work at all.

**Consequence for product direction:** International students and new arrivals are the segment for whom the status quo is worst and for whom a structured platform would provide the most transformative improvement. They are also a segment who would not find the platform through word-of-mouth — they would need to be reached through official university channels (international student office, orientation materials).

## Cross-Pattern Analysis

### Relationships Between Patterns

Pattern 1 (information invisibility) is the root cause from which all other patterns flow. Because no shared information layer exists for the Georgian tutoring market, students must contact tutors individually to discover basic facts — and this individual contact requirement directly produces Pattern 2 (the restart dynamic, because each contact that fails yields no residual information), Pattern 3 (the trust gap, because quality signals require an information infrastructure that does not exist), and Pattern 4 (bilateral coordination failure, because the same information absence affects tutors receiving demand as much as students seeking supply). Pattern 5 (network access inequality) is an amplification of Pattern 1 — it describes what Pattern 1 looks like for students whose social graph has never contained relevant tutor information in the first place.

Patterns 2 and 3 are sequential in the student experience: a student first runs through the restart loop (Pattern 2) until they either find someone or give up, and the conversion moment — when they actually commit to a tutor — is governed by trust (Pattern 3). Solving Pattern 1 without solving Pattern 3 would produce a platform that surfaces tutor names but does not convert students into booked sessions, because the trust mechanism (personal vouching) currently substitutes for the quality signal that a platform review system would provide.

Pattern 4 is the supply-side constraint on any solution. If tutors do not maintain profiles, the platform has no supply. MT's articulation of what she would want — "I have two spots left, I teach second-year maths, I charge 35 GEL per hour, then people who fit can message me" — is both a product specification and a clear statement of what would need to be true for a tutor to adopt the platform: it must reduce her coordination burden, not add to it.

### What the Data Says About Our Problem

The data confirms that the core problem is structural market failure, not individual user behaviour. Eight students and one active tutor, across different programmes, years, and national backgrounds, all encountered the same system and all described the same failure modes. No interviewee described a workaround that was adequate — the best outcome available (personal vouching by a trusted contact) is high-converting but inaccessible to anyone without the right social connection, non-reproducible, and non-scalable. The problem does not improve with experience: GM, the most experienced searcher in the dataset, still contacts four people before a match. The failure is in the infrastructure, not in the users.

### Implications for Product Direction

The top patterns together define a minimum viable solution more precisely than our original ICP did. A product that solves Pattern 1 alone (makes tutors findable) without Pattern 3 (provides a trust signal) will not convert. A product that solves Patterns 1 and 3 for students without addressing Pattern 4 (giving tutors a reason to maintain a profile) will have no supply. The evidence points toward a two-sided platform with three core functions: structured tutor profiles (solving Pattern 1), a social proof or review layer (solving Pattern 3), and a tutor-facing availability and filtering tool (solving Pattern 4 and creating supply-side adoption incentive). Pattern 5 suggests that the initial go-to-market should include an active channel to international students — they are the highest-pain segment and the least likely to discover the platform organically.
