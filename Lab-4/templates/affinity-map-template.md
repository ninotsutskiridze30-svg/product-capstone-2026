## Cluster Details

### Cluster 1: Students cannot access tutor information without starting a separate conversation for each candidate

**Theme:** Every data point a student needs to evaluate a tutor — availability, current rate, subject specialisation, and quality — is invisible until the student initiates a separate manual conversation. There is no persistent, shared record of any tutor's current state.

**Sample insights:**

1. "Everything. I did not know if they were any good, I did not know their rate, I did not know when they were free. You find out all of that by messaging them." (Interview 1, TK)
2. "I would see their name, what they teach, what they charge, some reviews maybe. And I would message one person and it would be done." (Interview 2, AB — describing the ideal, unprompted)
3. "Rates change, availability changes, nothing is written down anywhere. Every time you start a search it is like starting from scratch because nothing you knew before is still true." (Interview 3, GM)
4. "She gave me a name but told me the tutor only speaks Georgian. I do not speak Georgian well enough for a maths lesson." (Interview 4, SN — rate and availability are not the only invisible dimensions; language is too)
5. "I get messages from people who want subjects I do not teach, or who cannot afford my rate, or who need a time I do not have. It wastes both our time." (Interview 5, MT — the same information gap from the tutor's side)
6. Observation: TK said "there is no other way to find out" without hesitation — the absence of an alternative is already normalised (Interview 1)
7. SN spent time searching Georgian and Russian websites before concluding nothing existed for their context (Interview 4)

**Initial observation:** This is the largest and most consistent cluster across all five interviews. The information gap is not partial — it is total. A student who knows a tutor's name knows nothing actionable until they initiate contact. This validates the core of our committed problem statement and confirms that the solution requires a persistent, accessible information layer, not just a better way to ask in group chats.

---

### Cluster 2: Each failed contact resets the search to zero — there is no narrowing, only restarting

**Theme:** The search has no memory and no structure. When a contact fails — because the tutor is full, has changed their rate, does not reply, or teaches the wrong specialisation — the student does not move to a filtered next option. They return to the same channel with the same limited pool of names.

**Sample insights:**

1. "Two were full, one had changed their rate to something I could not afford, one just did not reply. The fourth one worked out." (Interview 3, GM)
2. "The whole thing took about a week and a half." (Interview 1, TK — describing finding one tutor for one subject)
3. "I do not know what to do next." (Interview 2, AB — currently mid-search with no remaining contacts to try)
4. "I did not make a decision to stop. I just stopped. There was nobody left to ask." (Interview 4, SN — search exhaustion as abandonment)
5. "I failed the first attempt. I passed the resit." (Interview 4, SN — the documented academic consequence of search failure)
6. "If my friend had not given me the last name directly... I would have stopped." (Interview 1, TK — abandonment was one step away)
7. "My roommate just gave up and went without." (Interview 1, TK — describing a peer who did not find a tutor)
8. GM described contacting four people before a match — and considered himself experienced at the process (Interview 3)

**Initial observation:** Abandonment is a real and common outcome, not a hypothetical. SN's failed exam is the most severe documented consequence, but TK nearly abandoned and TK's roommate did abandon. The restart dynamic — returning to a depleted channel rather than narrowing — is the structural reason searches take so long and fail so often. This cluster is the clearest evidence that the problem has direct academic consequences, not just time costs.

### Cluster 3: Trust and personal vouching are the conversion mechanism — a name without endorsement is not enough

**Theme:** Students do not act on a name alone. Conversion — actually messaging a tutor and committing to a session — requires a signal of credibility that goes beyond availability data. Personal vouching by someone who has used the tutor is the most reliable conversion mechanism currently available.

**Sample insights:**

1. "If my friend had not given me the last name directly and said she had used her personally, I would have stopped. I just needed someone to vouch for them." (Interview 1, TK)
2. "Someone gave me a name but they were not sure if that person still tutored." (Interview 4, SN — a name without confidence in its currency has low value)
3. AB described wanting "reviews maybe" as part of an ideal profile — unprompted, before any mention of a solution (Interview 2)
4. TK said they asked three separate people after finding the tutor whether 30 GEL per hour was a fair rate — they had no reference point and needed social confirmation even after the match was made (Interview 1)
5. GM's tutor was accepted because GM's friend had used her and vouched for her directly — the same mechanism as TK (Interview 3)
6. MT noted that students who are declined ask "do you know anyone else" — they are seeking a vouched referral, not just another name (Interview 5)

**Initial observation:** This cluster partially contradicts our original problem framing. We assumed the problem was discoverability — students cannot find tutors. The data shows it is also a trust problem — students who receive a name without vouching are not meaningfully better off. A platform that surfaces tutor names without social proof (reviews, shared connections, verified student status) addresses only half the gap. The trust layer is not a nice-to-have; it is part of the minimum viable solution.

### Cluster 4: Tutors are equally information-poor — they cannot signal availability and receive redundant mismatched inbound messages

**Theme:** The market failure is bilateral. Tutors spend significant time individually declining students who contact them — students they cannot take because they are full, because the rate does not match, or because the subject is wrong — because there is no coordination layer that lets either side filter before contact.

**Sample insights:**

1. "Every week I get messages from people I do not know. Maybe four or five a week, sometimes more before exams. Most of them I have to say no to." (Interview 5, MT)
2. "I am guessing just like they are." (Interview 5, MT — describing trying to refer a declined student to another tutor, with no more information than the student has)
3. "I would like to say somewhere: I have two spots left, I teach second-year maths, I charge 35 GEL per hour. And then people who fit that can message me." (Interview 5, MT — direct product specification, unprompted)
4. "I get the same message twice from two different people who do not know the other one already messaged me." (Interview 5, MT — duplication as evidence of zero market coordination)
5. Last month MT had a timing miss — a student who was a perfect fit messaged one day after a slot was claimed. Match failure due to sequencing, not incompatibility (Interview 5)
6. GM's tutor told him she wishes she could "put a status somewhere that says she is full" (Interview 3 — tutor-side wish surfaced via a student interview)

**Initial observation:** This cluster emerged entirely from one interview (MT) plus one proxy reference (GM). It is the most important finding that was not in our original problem statement. The supply side of the problem is real, felt, and directly symmetrical with the demand side. Any solution that only addresses student discovery leaves tutors — the supply side of a two-sided market — with the same inbox noise and coordination cost they have today. We need more tutor-side interviews before Lab 4.

---

### Cluster 5: International students face a compounded version — smaller network and language barriers eliminate most of the accessible tutor pool

**Theme:** For students without a Georgian social network, the group-chat discovery process has no entry point. For students without Georgian language ability, the effective tutor pool is further reduced — even tutors who are discoverable may not be accessible in a language suitable for instruction.

**Sample insights:**

1. "She gave me a name but told me the tutor only speaks Georgian. I do not speak Georgian well enough for a maths lesson." (Interview 4, SN)
2. "I looked online. I found some websites but they were for Russia or Ukraine, not Georgia. One was in Georgian only. I could not even read the listings." (Interview 4, SN)
3. "I did not make a decision to stop. I just stopped. There was nobody left to ask." (Interview 4, SN — exhaustion without an alternative)
4. AB mentioned that two friends from Azerbaijan experienced the same problem last semester and one never found a tutor (Interview 2 — corroboration from a second source)

**Initial observation:** This cluster was not in our original ICP framing. We knew international students had smaller networks, but we had not modelled the language barrier as a compounding constraint. For SN, the problem was not slow — it was structurally impossible. A Georgian-market platform with English-language support addresses both the network problem (by making tutors visible to students who have no word-of-mouth starting point) and the language problem (by surfacing tutors who teach in English). This sub-segment may be the most motivated early adopter group precisely because their current alternative is nothing.

## Outliers and Surprises

Insights that did not fit into any cluster but may be important:

1. "I started going at 8am. I do not like mornings." — From the library study space problem in Team Spotlight's worked example, not our data. Included here as a reference contrast. *(Not applicable to our dataset.)*

2. TK mentioned that their tutor's rate (30 GEL/hour) felt uncertain — they had no reference point and checked with three people after agreeing (Interview 1). **Why it matters:** Pricing opacity is a secondary pain that affects both the search (students don't know if a rate is fair before committing) and the relationship (tutors undercharge because there is no market reference). This connects to the pricing problem from our Lab 1 problem statements and may be worth a dedicated question in v2.

3. MT set their own tutoring rate by asking one friend what they charged and matching it (Interview 5). **Why it matters:** Pricing information is as absent on the supply side as on the demand side. Tutors and students are both operating with no reference point. A platform that makes rates visible solves a pain on both sides simultaneously.

4. SN said "I never thought something like that would be available here" when asked about a structured search tool (Interview 4). **Why it matters:** The segment most harmed by the absence of a solution has the lowest expectation that one could exist. This has distribution implications — passive discovery (app stores, search) may not reach this group; active outreach through university channels might be necessary.

## Relationships Between Clusters

- **Cluster 1 is the root cause of Clusters 2, 3, and 4.** Because no tutor information is visible without individual contact, every search must proceed manually and serially (Cluster 2), every conversion requires a trust signal that substitutes for information (Cluster 3), and tutors cannot filter inbound interest before contact either (Cluster 4).

- **Cluster 2 and Cluster 3 are sequential.** A student who receives a name first faces the information gap (Cluster 1), then restarts after a failed contact (Cluster 2), and only converts when they receive a personal vouching signal (Cluster 3). The trust mechanism is the workaround for both information absence and restart exhaustion.

- **Cluster 5 is an amplified version of Cluster 2.** Domestic students run out of names after multiple restarts. International students have no first name — the starting pool is empty before the process begins.

- **Cluster 4 (tutor-side) is the mirror of Cluster 1 (student-side).** Students cannot see tutor information; tutors cannot broadcast their availability. The same absence of a shared coordination layer produces symmetric information poverty on both sides.

## Team Notes

**What surprised us:** The supply-side problem (Cluster 4) was not in our original framing and emerged only because Interview 3 (GM) mentioned his tutor's perspective offhand. One interview with an active tutor (MT) confirmed it completely. The bilateral nature of the market failure — tutors and students are both information-poor — significantly changes what a solution needs to do.

**What confirmed our assumptions:** Cluster 1 is exactly what we hypothesised in our problem statement and ICP. The information gap is total, affects every search, and is experienced identically by students regardless of year, programme, or background. The restart dynamic (Cluster 2) is also fully confirmed — experienced students (GM) contact 3–4 people; inexperienced ones (TK) take a week and a half; international students (SN) exhaust the pool entirely.

**What we need to investigate further:** Three open questions going into Lab 4: (1) Is search abandonment and academic failure (SN's outcome) representative or a severe outlier — we need more data before claiming it is typical. (2) Would tutors actively maintain a profile to reduce inbox noise — or is the coordination cost low enough that they would not adopt a new tool? (3) Does the trust requirement (Cluster 3) mean we need a formal review system, or would weaker signals (same university, mutual connection) be enough to convert a student?
