# Final Problem Statement

**Team:** TutorLink Team
**Date:** March 31, 2026
**Version:** 1.0
**Based on:** 8 interviews conducted between March 22 and March 27, 2026

---

## The Problem Statement

### Complete Statement

> University students in Georgia who need a private tutor cannot find, evaluate, or trust a suitable match without initiating a separate manual conversation for each candidate — a process that takes between one and six weeks, ends in abandonment for a significant minority, and in the most severe cases results in academic failure. The problem is structural and bilateral: students have no visibility into tutor availability, rate, subject specialisation, or quality before making contact, and tutors have no way to signal their status or filter incoming student demand, so both sides of the market operate with complete information poverty. The current workaround — posting in group chats, collecting names, and messaging each tutor individually — is the only available channel for most students, produces stale and unverifiable information, resets to zero with every failed contact, and is inaccessible to students without an established Georgian social network.

## Component Breakdown

### WHO (Ideal Customer Profile)

**Primary user:** University students at KIU and similar Georgian institutions who are actively searching for a private tutor in a specific subject and relying entirely on word-of-mouth channels to find one.

**Demographics:** Age 18–24. First, second, or third-year undergraduate students. Studying away from home, often without family guidance on finding academic support in Georgia. Searches are triggered by falling behind in a course or preparing for an exam — periods of heightened academic stress.

**Key characteristics that make them vulnerable to this problem:**

1. No prior relationship with a tutor — must search from scratch at exactly the moment they are already under academic pressure.
   Evidence: "The whole thing took about a week and a half." (Interview 1, TK — describing searching for one tutor for one subject while simultaneously falling further behind)

2. Entirely dependent on their social network as the discovery channel — students with smaller or newer networks have fewer names to try.
   Evidence: "I did not make a decision to stop. I just stopped. There was nobody left to ask." (Interview 4, SN — after six weeks of searching with no match)

3. International students face a compounded version: smaller network AND language barriers reduce the effective accessible tutor pool to near zero.
   Evidence: "She gave me a name but told me the tutor only speaks Georgian. I do not speak Georgian well enough for a maths lesson." (Interview 4, SN)

**How many people have this problem (estimated):** KIU enrols approximately 2,000–3,000 students. Based on our interviews, the search for a private tutor is a recurring need across programmes and years. If 30–40% of students search for a tutor at least once per academic year, the addressable population at KIU alone is 600–1,200 students annually. Across all Georgian universities with international programmes, the total is significantly larger.

---

### WHAT (The Problem)

**The core problem:** Students cannot access any information about a tutor — availability, rate, subject coverage, or quality — without initiating a separate manual conversation, and each failed conversation resets the entire search rather than narrowing it.

**How it manifests:**

1. Total information invisibility before contact — rate, availability, specialisation, and quality are all unknown until the student messages the tutor directly.
   Evidence: "Everything. I did not know if they were any good, I did not know their rate, I did not know when they were free. You find out all of that by messaging them." (Interview 1, TK)

2. Serial restart dynamic — each failed contact (full, wrong rate, no reply, wrong specialisation) produces no residual information and no path to the next candidate.
   Evidence: "Two were full, one had changed their rate to something I could not afford, one just did not reply. The fourth one worked out." (Interview 3, GM — an experienced searcher who still contacted four people before a match)

3. Visibility failure even at close social range — students are physically proximate to tutors but cannot identify them without an intermediary.
   Evidence: "I can see people in my year who tutor. I just don't know who they are or how to reach them." (Interview 8, LG)

**Frequency:** The problem occurs at two predictable peaks per year — semester start and the 4–6 weeks before final exams — and episodically whenever a student falls behind in a course. Every student who searches for a tutor encounters the problem; it is structural, not circumstantial.

**Evidence of frequency:** "Every week I get messages from people I do not know. Maybe four or five a week, sometimes more before exams." (Interview 5, MT — describing inbound student demand from the tutor's side, confirming search volume is sustained and peaks seasonally)

---

### WHEN / WHERE (Context)

**When does this problem occur?** At the moment a student decides they need a tutor — triggered by a failed exam, a missed assignment, or the recognition that a subject is beyond self-study. The search begins immediately and competes with the academic work the student is already behind on.

**Where does it happen?** Primarily on mobile, inside WhatsApp and Messenger group chats, in the evenings after classes. The search moves from group chats to individual DMs as names are collected, and back to group chats when individual contacts fail.

**Evidence:**

1. "I posted in the CS group chat. Someone gave me a name. I messaged that person, they said they were full. Someone else gave me another name." (Interview 1, TK — the channel sequence described step by step)

2. "I asked in my Business group chat. Nobody answered. I posted again three days later." (Interview 2, AB — the same channel, a different programme, the same failure mode)

---

### WHY (Root Cause)

**Root cause:** There is no shared, persistent, accessible information layer for the Georgian private tutoring market. Tutor status — availability, rate, subject, language — exists only in the tutor's head and changes continuously. Students have no mechanism to query it without direct contact, and tutors have no mechanism to broadcast it without responding to every individual inquiry. The market has no coordination infrastructure.

**Why existing solutions fail:**

1. WhatsApp/Messenger group chats fail because responses are inconsistent, names arrive without context, information is stale by the time it reaches the student, and each failed contact returns the student to an already-depleted pool with no new information.
   Evidence: "Rates change, availability changes, nothing is written down anywhere. Every time you start a search it is like starting from scratch because nothing you knew before is still true." (Interview 3, GM)

2. Facebook groups and online platforms fail because Georgian tutors are not listed on them, existing listings are inactive or language-inaccessible, and international platforms (Russian, Ukrainian tutoring sites) do not serve the Georgian market.
   Evidence: "I looked online. I found some websites but they were for Russia or Ukraine, not Georgia. One was in Georgian only. I could not even read the listings." (Interview 4, SN)

3. Personal vouching (the highest-converting channel) fails because it is available only to students with the right social connection, does not scale, and produces no residual information for future searchers.
   Evidence: "If my friend had not given me the last name directly and said she had used her personally, I would have stopped. I just needed someone to vouch for them." (Interview 1, TK — the one mechanism that worked, available only by luck)

---

### IMPACT (Consequences)

**Quantified impact:**

- Time lost: 1–6 weeks per tutor search — Evidence: Interviews 1 (TK: ~1.5 weeks), 3 (GM: ~2 weeks across four contacts), 4 (SN: 6 weeks before abandonment)
- Academic cost: Failed first exam attempt with documented direct causation — Evidence: Interview 4 (SN: "I failed the first attempt. I passed the resit.")
- Tutor-side time lost: 4–5 manual decline messages per week plus time spent trying to refer declined students — Evidence: Interview 5 (MT)
- Emotional cost: Anxiety about quality before the first paid session, resignation to the search as a fact of life — Evidence: "The worst part is you don't know if the person is any good until you've already committed to a session and paid for it." (Interview 6, DK)
- Opportunity cost: Students fall further behind in the subject during the search period because the course does not pause while they look — Evidence: "You fall further behind while you are trying to catch up." (Interview 3, GM)

**Aggregate impact:** If 600 KIU students search for a tutor annually, and the average search takes 1–2 weeks of active effort across multiple conversations, the aggregate student time lost to tutor discovery is approximately 600–1,200 student-weeks per year — before accounting for the compounding academic cost of the delay. On the tutor side, MT alone declines 4–5 students per week, investing time in conversations that produce no match. Across dozens of active tutors in the KIU network, the total coordination cost is substantial.

## Pivot Decision

- [ ] **No pivot needed.** Interview data validates our original hypothesis. Proceeding as planned.
- [x] **Minor refinement.** The core problem is the same but our understanding has sharpened. Adjusting ICP and scope.
- [ ] **Major pivot.** The data revealed a different problem is more significant.

**Refinement explanation:** Our original problem statement focused entirely on the student-side discovery problem. The interviews — particularly Interview 5 (MT) and the supply-side references in Interview 3 (GM) — revealed that the problem is bilateral: tutors experience symmetric information poverty and coordination cost. Our revised statement explicitly names the bilateral nature of the failure. The ICP remains the same (student searcher as primary, parent as secondary) but we are now tracking a third ICP candidate: the active private tutor who receives redundant inbound demand. This does not change our problem commitment — it deepens it. We are not pivoting to build a tutor tool; we are recognising that a student-facing solution must also create value for tutors or it will not have supply.

Additionally, Interview 7 (NA) surfaced the cross-university tutor dynamic — tutors in Tbilisi often serve students from multiple institutions. Our original framing assumed the tutor pool was KIU-specific. The real supply is wider, which is an opportunity, not a complication.
