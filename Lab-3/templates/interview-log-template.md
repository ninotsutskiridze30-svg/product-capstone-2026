## Verbatim Quotes

**Quote 1**
Context: asked "Walk me through what you did from the moment you decided you needed a maths tutor"

> "I posted in the CS group chat. Someone gave me a name. I messaged that person, they said they were full. Someone else gave me another name. That person was available but only on Sunday mornings, which I cannot do. Then I posted again because I had run out of names. The whole thing took about a week and a half."

What this tells us: The search is entirely sequential — each failed contact requires restarting, not narrowing. A week and a half for a single subject confirms the time cost we hypothesised. The participant did not exhaust one channel before moving to another — they returned to the same channel (group chat) because there was no other option.

---

**Quote 2**
Context: asked "Was there anything you wanted to know about the tutor before messaging them that you could not find out without contacting them directly?"

> "Everything. I did not know if they were any good, I did not know their rate, I did not know when they were free. You find out all of that by messaging them. And then if something does not work you have to start again from zero."

What this tells us: The information gap is total — not partial. Rate, availability, quality, and subject coverage all require a separate conversation. "Start again from zero" captures the restart dynamic precisely.

---

**Quote 3**
Context: asked "How did it feel by the end of the process?"

> "Honestly I almost gave up. If my friend had not given me the last name directly and said she had used her personally, I would have stopped. I just needed someone to vouch for them."

What this tells us: Trust is a critical dimension we underweighted in our original ICP. A personal vouching function — someone who has used this tutor and is willing to say so — was what converted the search into a successful outcome. This is not just a discoverability problem; it is a trust problem.

---

**Quote 4**
Context: off-script follow-up — asked "What did other students you know do when they needed a tutor?"

> "My roommate just gave up and went without. He said the search was not worth it. He has an exam in May and I think he regrets it now."

What this tells us: Abandonment is a real outcome, not a hypothetical. Students who cannot find a tutor quickly enough do not keep searching indefinitely — they exit the market. This has direct academic consequences.

---

## Key Observations

- TK became visibly frustrated when recounting the Sunday morning scheduling conflict — this was the moment where the process felt most wasteful to them
- When asked if they thought about looking outside personal networks (e.g. a website or app), they said "that doesn't exist here" without hesitation — the assumption that a structured alternative does not exist is already baked in
- TK mentioned the tutor they ultimately found charges 30 GEL per hour — they had no idea if this was fair until they asked three separate people afterward

---

## Preliminary Insights

- The search failure mode is not finding the wrong tutor — it is running out of names and not knowing where to get more. The group chat is a single node; when it is exhausted, the student has nowhere to go.
- Trust (personal vouching) is the conversion mechanism. A name without a voucher has low conversion value. This suggests that a solution needs to carry some form of social proof, not just availability data.
- Students who abandon the search do not announce it — they quietly exit. This means the true cost of the fragmented market is larger than what is visible in the network.

## Interviewer Reflection

Most important learning: Trust (personal vouching) is as important as information availability. A list of tutors without social proof would not have converted TK — they specifically needed someone to say "I used her and she was good."

What to do differently: The question "how did it feel by the end of the process" was not in our v1 script and produced the most emotionally honest answer. Add it to v2.
